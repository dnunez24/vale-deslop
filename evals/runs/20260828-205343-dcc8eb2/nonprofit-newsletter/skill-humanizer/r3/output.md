# Clearwater Relief: fall update for our supporters

Clearwater Relief passed 500 wells this season. They sit in four countries and serve roughly 120,000 people. Every well we drill changes a family's entire future, and 500 of them is a milestone worth telling you about.

## What we accomplished this season

Crossing 500 will change how three regions think about clean water access. Well #500 defines this chapter of Clearwater's story the way well #1 defined the first one.

Our field teams worked through the dry season and finished twelve wells in a single month. When a household can reach clean water within a fifteen-minute walk, school attendance changes, and it changes most for girls. Every dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in reduced healthcare costs.

Clean water changes everything for a village. Every community we've worked with, without exception, has asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

Whether you've given monthly for years or gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season.

## Stories from the field

Numbers don't capture what changed in Mbita village this year. The season was hard before the rains finally came, and I won't pretend otherwise.

Water used to mean a two-hour walk and missed school days. Now it means a five-minute walk and a full day of class.

The well, finished in March, serves 400 people. Local technicians trained through our program keep it working. A maintenance fund seeded by this year's donors covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team, a sentiment we now hear in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

Her daughters got their school year back, which is what a five-minute walk buys a family. Other villages are still waiting for the same thing.

## Looking ahead

Our field coordinator is the bridge between headquarters and every village crew. The maintenance fund is our insurance against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious yet, built on three years of lessons about what actually keeps a well running.

The roadmap runs planning, drilling, and training in a single dry season, village by village. It is simple and ambitious at once, lightweight and still durable enough to survive a decade of hard use.

We're aiming at clarity, accountability, and reach in every region we serve.

Some partners have called the approach "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Our field data over three years says otherwise.

We're ready, we're funded, and we're moving.

We've picked the sites, trained the crews, and ordered the materials. Drilling starts in January.

## Where your support goes

Your gift covers three real categories:

- **Drilling:** rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- **Training:** local technicians who keep every well running for decades
- **Monitoring:** the follow-up visits that catch a failing pump before it fails

That's how a one-time gift turns into decades of clean water, and the model is efficient because it's simple: drill once, train locally, monitor forever. It's lightweight and still complete, so every village gets exactly the support it needs to keep a well running long after we leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |
| Monitoring    | $180K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it funded every well this season. Training costs less and matters just as much, since it's what keeps a well running past year one. Monitoring is the smallest budget of the three, and it caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well, grew into a four-country program, and now other nonprofits copy the model. Three lessons got us here. Drilling without training fails within a year. Monitoring catches problems that training alone misses. And local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
