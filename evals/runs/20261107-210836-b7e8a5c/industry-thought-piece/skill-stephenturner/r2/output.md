# The Great Reskilling: What Every Company Gets Wrong About AI Adoption

Executives used to debate whether to adopt artificial intelligence. Now they compete over how fast they can claim they already have. Read a few of these strategies side by side and you'll notice they match word for word, quarter after quarter.

## Everyone Is Copying the Same Playbook

I expect this shift to change how whole industries think about labor. AI adoption will shape this decade of business much as the internet shaped the last one.

Companies that automate keep running into the same problem: each new round of automation uncovers work nobody had written down. Most executives still bring the same three-slide deck to every board meeting. Investors want an AI strategy, so companies produce one even when the underlying business hasn't changed at all.

Adopting AI matters less than matching a specific tool to a specific problem. The companies that win here owe their results to process work nobody wanted to do first, and much less to the tool they bought.

Executives talk as if AI adoption is inevitable. Every executive I've interviewed this year, without exception, has said "transformative technology" within the first five minutes.

Adoption metrics and board confidence both rose, while real usage flatlined.

Most of these numbers come from the vendor running the pilot, not from an independent auditor. The industry's approach is simple and cynical, and it sounds confident about results nobody has proven at the scale vendors claim. The pitch puts speed first, certainty second and scale third, whatever the actual project happens to be.

Slide one shows the same chart every time, and slide two shows the same testimonial. The third slide carries the same roadmap as last year, with every date pushed back a year.

## The Buzzword Problem

Vendors are selling a growing pile of AI tools, each promising big benefits to any company willing to dig through its own data. In their telling, every company's data warehouse holds valuable insights that a wave of new algorithms will unlock.

Nearly every vendor's homepage says its product is new, top of its class, and able to connect features across every department. That positioning runs on ambition, urgency and fear in roughly equal measure.

Most buyers never look inside the vendor's platform. For most executives, the sales deck is the only technical documentation they ever read. A pilot program proves nothing except that a demo worked once.

You have probably watched this happen at your own company, since nearly every company is chasing the same headline. Maybe the bottleneck was never the technology. That possibility should worry procurement teams who've spent the last two years buying licenses instead of building process.

## What Actually Works

Studies have shown that companies with a clear, narrow use case outperform companies with a broad AI mandate by a wide margin. The winning pattern is automation aimed at one specific, well-understood bottleneck, and experts agree that piling on more automation doesn't produce it.

The companies seeing real returns appear to have started with a narrow pilot instead of a company-wide rollout. The data suggests scope discipline may matter more than model quality, though both could matter and the sample is too small to separate them yet. The pattern might hold across industries, and you could argue scope explains nearly all of it.

Adoption succeeded where scope stayed narrow and stalled where it crept. Where nobody owned the outcome, it failed outright.

Our own approach was plain. We picked one workflow and measured the baseline, then shipped a narrow tool and expanded only after it worked.

Companies tend to treat AI adoption as a procurement decision when it is really a process decision. They measure pilots by activity rather than outcomes. And they roll out company-wide before a single team has shown the workflow works.

## The Human Side Nobody Talks About

Reskilling takes years, and most companies haven't budgeted for it. In my experience, most "reskilling initiatives" amount to a single afternoon webinar and a certificate nobody asked for.

I hope this piece helps you see past the vendor talking points. If there's interest, a follow-up could look at how three companies built a reskilling program that worked.

Some questions rarely get asked. Nobody seems to plan for the people whose job was the workflow that just got automated. Few companies budget for retraining before the rollout rather than after, and I can't say when "reskilling" will become more than a talking point. I also don't know how many companies have measured whether it worked.

Some labor economists have called the reskilling narrative "corporate theater" designed to soften layoff announcements, and the data on retraining outcomes mostly backs them up.

Three questions worth asking before the next board meeting:

- Scope: does this project touch one workflow or every workflow at once
- Ownership: does one team own the outcome, or does everyone own a slice of it
- Measurement: will anyone know, in six months, whether this actually worked

| Adoption Pattern    | Scope   | Typical Outcome |
| -------------------- | ------- | ----------------- |
| Company-wide mandate | Broad   | Stalls within a year |
| Narrow pilot         | Single team | Expands after proof |
| Vendor-led rollout   | Broad   | Adoption theater |

These answers decide whether a project ships or ends up as a talking point on next year's earnings call, which is why they deserve more than a spreadsheet.

## Where This Leaves Us

The companies that will look smart in five years are being boring about AI right now. They keep scope narrow, measure honestly, and have a real plan for the people whose work changes. Robots replacing workers makes a better headline, but the real story is which companies bothered to do the unglamorous process work first.

I doubt the winners will be the loudest companies. The best AI story next year will probably come from a company nobody's heard of yet.

Every vendor, every conference keynote and now every earnings call insists the industry has reached its breakthrough moment.

The technology was never the hard part. The hard part, which nobody wants on a slide, is the years of unglamorous process work most companies are still avoiding.

The industry prizes speed, scale and ambition, though a single project rarely delivers all three.

I've seen the same thing in industry after industry. The loudest AI announcement rarely comes from the team that made it work. The quiet teams, with no press release, no keynote slot and no vendor logo on their homepage, are usually six months ahead of everyone else.
