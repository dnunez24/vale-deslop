# Clearwater Relief: Fall Update for Our Supporters

This season Clearwater Relief finished its 500th well. Those wells now span four countries and serve roughly 120,000 people, and for each family on the receiving end, a well near home changes what the coming years look like.

## What We Accomplished This Season

We expect these 500 wells to change how three regions plan for clean water. For Clearwater, well #500 marks the start of a new chapter, much as well #1 marked the start of the first.

The dry season was hard on our field teams, and they still finished twelve wells in a single month. Experts agree that when clean water sits within a fifteen-minute walk, school attendance changes, especially for girls. Studies have shown that every dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in reduced healthcare costs.

Clean water changes a village in ways people already understand. Every community we've worked with has asked us for the same things: a well that stays reliable, training for the people who run it, and local ownership once we leave.

If you've given monthly for years, or if you gave for the first time this year, your money reached a real family this season. 💧

## Stories From the Field

The numbers leave out most of what changed in Mbita village this year. I also want to be honest that this season was hard until the rains finally came.

Getting water in Mbita used to take a two-hour walk, and girls missed school to make it. Now the walk takes five minutes and they stay in class all day.

The well, finished in March, serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working. This year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team now hears something close to that in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

Her daughters' story raises things we keep thinking about: why five minutes of walking matters so much, how long it has been since the village went without water, and how many other villages are waiting for the same thing.

## Looking Ahead

Our field coordinator connects headquarters with every village crew. We set up the maintenance fund as insurance against the one thing that could undo a season of work, a pump nobody can afford to fix. Next year's plan is our most ambitious so far, and it draws on three years of learning what actually keeps a well running.

Next year we plan to go from planning to drilling to training within a single dry season, working village by village. The approach is simple, though the wells have to hold up through a decade of hard use.

In every region we serve, we want the plan to be clear and to hold us accountable while reaching more people.

Some partners have told us the timeline is "unrealistic." Our field data from the past three years says otherwise.

We're ready, and the work is funded.

The sites are picked and the crews are trained. Materials are on order, and drilling starts in January.

## Where Your Support Goes

Your gift goes to three areas:

- Drilling: rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- Training: local technicians who keep each well running for decades
- Monitoring: follow-up visits that catch a weak pump before it fails

A one-time gift turns into decades of clean water this way. We drill once, train people locally, and keep monitoring after that. The model is small, but it gives each village what it needs to keep a well running long after our crews leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |
| Monitoring    | $180K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less, but it kept every well running past year one. Monitoring was the smallest budget of the three, and our monitors still caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well and grew into a four-country program whose model other nonprofits now copy. Along the way we learned that wells drilled without training fail within a year. Monitoring then caught problems that training alone missed. Local ownership turned out to be the only thing that lasts.
