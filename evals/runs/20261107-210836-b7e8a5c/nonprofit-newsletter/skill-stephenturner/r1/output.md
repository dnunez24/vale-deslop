# Clearwater Relief: Fall Update for Our Supporters

This season Clearwater Relief finished its 500th well. Those wells are spread across four countries and serve roughly 120,000 people, and we wanted you to hear the number from us first.

## What We Accomplished This Season

We expect this work to change how three regions plan for clean water. For us, well #500 marks the start of a new chapter, much as well #1 marked the start of the first.

The dry season was hard on our field teams, and they still finished twelve wells in a single month. Water researchers have found that when clean water sits within a fifteen-minute walk, girls in particular attend school more often. Studies have also shown that each dollar spent on water infrastructure saves four dollars in healthcare costs.

Ask any village what a working well does and you'll hear a long answer. When we ask what they need from us, though, every community we've worked with has named the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

If you give every month, or if you gave for the first time this year, your money reached a real family this season.

## Stories From the Field

The numbers leave out what happened in Mbita village this year. I'll also admit this season was hard until the rains finally came.

A trip for water used to take two hours on foot, and kids missed school to make it. Now the walk takes five minutes, and they stay in class all day.

We finished the well in March, and it serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working, and this year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team hears some version of that in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

We still want to understand more: what exactly shifted for those girls, how long the village had gone without water before, and how many other villages are waiting for the same thing.

## Looking Ahead

Our field coordinator links headquarters with every village crew. The maintenance fund protects against the one thing that could undo a season of work, a well that breaks and stays broken. Next year's plan is our most ambitious so far, and it draws on three years of lessons about what keeps a well running.

In a single dry season, each village moves through planning, drilling, and training before the crews move on to the next. We've kept the plan simple, and we've built it to survive a decade of hard use.

In every region we serve, we want the plan to be clear, accountable, and wide in reach.

Some partners have called the plan "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Our field data from the past three years says otherwise.

The money is in place and the work is underway.

We've picked the sites, trained the crews, and ordered the materials. Drilling starts in January.

## Where Your Support Goes

Your gift goes to three things:

- Drilling: rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- Training: local technicians who keep every well running for decades
- Monitoring: follow-up visits that catch a failing pump before it breaks down

The model is simple. We drill once, train people locally, and keep monitoring, so a one-time gift keeps water flowing for decades. Each village gets the support it needs to run its well long after our crews leave.

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less, but it kept every well running past year one. Monitoring is the smallest budget of the three, and our monitors caught eleven failing pumps before they gave out completely.

We started with a single well, grew into a four-country program, and now see other nonprofits copying our model. Early on, we found that wells drilled without training fail within a year. Later we saw that monitoring catches problems training alone misses. Over time we came to believe local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
