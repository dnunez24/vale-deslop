# Clearwater Relief: Fall Update for Our Supporters

This season Clearwater Relief finished its 500th well. Those wells, spread across four countries, now serve roughly 120,000 people, and we wanted you to hear the number from us first.

## What We Accomplished This Season

We expect this milestone to change how three regions plan for clean water. Well #500 marks a new chapter for Clearwater, much as well #1 marked the first.

The dry season made drilling hard, and our field teams still finished twelve wells in a single month. Research on water access points to girls in particular: when clean water sits within a fifteen-minute walk, their school attendance changes. Studies have also found that each dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in lower healthcare costs.

In our experience, a village with clean water runs differently. The communities we've worked with have asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

If you've given monthly for years, or gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season. 💧

## Stories From the Field

The numbers leave out what changed in Mbita village this year. I also want to be honest that this season was hard until the rains finally came.

Getting water in Mbita used to take a two-hour walk, and girls missed school to make it. Now the walk takes five minutes and they stay in class all day.

We finished the well in March, and it serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working, and this year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team hears something close to that in most villages we visit. She told them her daughters had never gone a full week without missing school until this year.

We think about the other villages still waiting for a well like hers, and about how long Mbita went without one.

## Looking Ahead

Our field coordinator connects headquarters with each village crew. The maintenance fund protects against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious yet, and it draws on three years of lessons about what keeps a well running.

Next year we plan to drill and then train in a single dry season, one village at a time. The plan is ambitious, and the wells have to survive a decade of hard use.

For each region we serve, we're aiming for clear reporting and wider reach, and we'll hold ourselves accountable to both.

Some partners have called the approach "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Our field data from the past three years says otherwise.

The work is funded and ready to go.

We've picked the sites, trained the crews, and ordered materials, and drilling starts in January.

## Where Your Support Goes

Your gift breaks down into three real categories:

- **Drilling:** rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- **Training:** local technicians who keep each well running for decades
- **Monitoring:** follow-up visits that catch a weak pump before it breaks down

A one-time gift becomes decades of clean water because we drill once, train people locally, and keep monitoring. That combination gives a village what it needs to keep its well running long after our crews leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |

| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less, but it kept the wells running past year one. Monitoring is the smallest budget of the three, and those visits caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well, grew into a four-country program, and now see other nonprofits copying our model. Early on we learned that wells drilled without training fail within a year. Later we found that monitoring catches problems training misses, and that local ownership is what lasts.
