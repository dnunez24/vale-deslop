# Clearwater Relief: Fall Update for Our Supporters

This season Clearwater Relief finished its 500th well. The program now reaches four countries and serves roughly 120,000 people, and each new source changes what the future looks like for the families who draw from it.

## What We Accomplished This Season

We expect this milestone to change how three regions think about clean water access. Well #1 opened the first chapter of Clearwater's story, and well #500 opens this one.

The dry season was hard on our field teams, and they still finished twelve wells in a single month. That pace matters. Experts agree that having clean water within a fifteen-minute walk changes school attendance, for girls in particular. Studies have shown that every dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in reduced healthcare costs.

Clean water changes a lot for a village. Every community we've worked with has asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

If you've given monthly for years, or gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season. 💧

## Stories From the Field

The numbers leave out what changed in Mbita village this year. They also leave out how hard the season was before the rains finally came, and I want to be honest about that.

In Mbita, water used to mean a two-hour walk and missed school days. Now it means a five-minute walk and a full day of class.

The well, finished in March, serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working. This year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team hears something like it in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

Her story leaves us with questions we keep asking. What changed for her daughters, and why does a five-minute walk matter so much? When did the village last go without water? And how many other villages are waiting for the same thing?

## Looking Ahead

Our field coordinator links headquarters with every village crew. The maintenance fund insures against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious so far, and it draws on three years of lessons about what keeps a well running.

The roadmap takes each village from planning to drilling to training within a single dry season. It's ambitious, but the steps are simple, and the wells are built to survive a decade of hard use.

In every region we serve, the plan aims for clarity, accountability, and reach.

Some partners have called the approach "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Three years of our field data says otherwise.

We're ready, funded, and moving.

The sites are picked, the crews trained, and the materials ordered. Drilling starts in January.

## Where Your Support Goes

Your gift goes to three categories:

- **Drilling:** rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- **Training:** local technicians who keep each well running for decades
- **Monitoring:** follow-up visits that catch a failing pump before it fails

The model is simple: drill once, train locally, monitor forever. That's how a one-time gift becomes decades of clean water. It gives each village the support it needs to keep a well running long after we leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |
| Monitoring    | $180K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less but kept every well running past year one. Monitoring, the smallest budget of the three, caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well, grew into a four-country program, and now see other nonprofits copying our model. We learned three things on the way. First, drilling without training fails within a year. Second, monitoring catches the problems that training alone misses, like a pump that's starting to fail. Third, local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
