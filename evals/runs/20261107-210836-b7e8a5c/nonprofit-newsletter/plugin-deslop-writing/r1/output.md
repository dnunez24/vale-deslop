# Clearwater Relief: Fall Update for Our Supporters

This season Clearwater Relief finished its 500th well. Those wells now span four countries and serve roughly 120,000 people, and each one changes what the next few decades look like for the families who use it.

## What We Accomplished This Season

We expect the 500th well to change how three regions approach clean water access. Well #1 started the first chapter of Clearwater's story; well #500 marks the start of this one.

The dry season was hard on our field teams, and they still finished twelve wells in a single month. That pace matters because a water source within a fifteen-minute walk changes school attendance, especially for girls. Experts agree on that point. Studies have also found that each dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in lower healthcare costs.

Clean water changes a village. Every community we've worked with, without exception, has asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

If you give monthly and have for years, or if you gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season.

## Stories From the Field

The numbers leave out what changed in Mbita village this year. I also want to be honest that this season was hard right up until the rains came.

Water in Mbita used to mean a two-hour walk and missed school days. Now it's a five-minute walk, and children stay in class all day.

The well was finished in March and serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working, and this year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team now hears some version of that in almost every village it visits. She said her daughters had never before gone a full week without missing school.

Her daughters' week at school is one answer to why a five-minute walk matters so much. We still ask when the village last went without water, and how many other villages are waiting for the same thing.

## Looking Ahead

Our field coordinator links headquarters with every village crew. The maintenance fund protects against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious so far, and it draws on three years of lessons about what keeps a well running.

The roadmap covers planning, drilling, and training in a single dry season, one village at a time. It's a simple plan with big targets, and the wells it builds are meant to survive a decade of hard use.

In every region we serve, the plan aims for clarity, accountability, and reach.

Some partners have called it "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Three years of our field data say otherwise.

The plan is funded and ready to go.

The sites are picked, the crews trained, and the materials ordered. Drilling starts in January.

## Where Your Support Goes

Your gift goes to three things:

- **Drilling:** rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- **Training:** local technicians who keep each well running for decades
- **Monitoring:** follow-up visits that catch a weak pump before it breaks down

That's how a one-time gift becomes decades of clean water: drill once, train locally, monitor for good. The model has no wasted pieces. It gives each village what it needs to keep a well running long after we leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |
| Monitoring    | $180K      | 500               |

Drilling was our largest line item and paid for every well this season. Training cost less, but it kept every well running past its first year. Monitoring was the smallest of the three, and it caught eleven failing pumps before they quit.

We started with a single well. We grew into a four-country program, and other nonprofits now copy our model. Along the way we learned three things. Drilling without training fails within a year, and monitoring catches problems that training alone misses. Above all, local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
