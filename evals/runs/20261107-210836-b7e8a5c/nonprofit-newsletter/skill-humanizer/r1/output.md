# Clearwater Relief: Fall update for our supporters

Every well we drill changes what's possible for a family. This season Clearwater Relief reached 500 wells across four countries, serving roughly 120,000 people, and we're glad to be able to tell you about it.

## What we accomplished this season

We expect this to change how three regions approach clean water access. Well #500 matters to this part of Clearwater's history the way well #1 mattered to the start of it.

The dry season was hard, but our field teams still finished twelve wells in a single month. Water within a fifteen-minute walk is widely understood to change school attendance, especially for girls. Studies have also found that every dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in reduced healthcare costs.

Clean water changes a lot for a village. Every community we've worked with, without exception, has asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

If you've given monthly for years, or if you gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season.

## Stories from the field

The numbers don't capture what changed in Mbita village this year. I also want to be honest about how hard this season was before the rains finally came.

Water used to mean a two-hour walk and missed school days. Now it means a five-minute walk and a full day of class.

The well, finished in March, serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working, and this year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team now hears something like that in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

That raises questions we keep asking ourselves. What changed for her daughters? Why does a five-minute walk matter so much? When did the village last go without water? And how many other villages are waiting for the same thing?

## Looking ahead

Our field coordinator links headquarters with every village crew. The maintenance fund is our insurance against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious yet, and it draws on three years of lessons about what actually keeps a well running.

Next year we plan to move each village from planning to drilling to training within a single dry season. The approach is simple, but it's ambitious, and we've built it to be light enough to move quickly and durable enough to survive a decade of hard use.

In every region we serve, the plan aims for clarity, accountability, and reach.

Some partners have called the approach "unrealistic" for such a short timeline. Our field data from the past three years says otherwise.

We're ready, funded, and moving.

The sites are picked, the crews are trained, and the materials are ordered. Drilling starts in January.

## Where your support goes

Your gift goes to three things:

- **Drilling:** rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- **Training:** local technicians who keep each well running for decades
- **Monitoring:** follow-up visits that catch a failing pump before it fails

This is how a one-time gift becomes decades of clean water: drill once, train locally, and keep monitoring. The model doesn't carry much overhead, but it covers what each village needs to keep a well running long after we leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |
| Monitoring    | $180K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less, but it kept every well running past year one. Monitoring is the smallest of the three, and it caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well. We now run a program in four countries, and other nonprofits copy the model. Along the way we learned three things. Drilling without training fails within a year. Monitoring catches problems that training alone misses. And local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
