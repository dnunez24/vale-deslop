# Clearwater Relief: Fall update for our supporters

A new well can change a family's future, and the world those families live in is fragile. This season Clearwater Relief reached a milestone we're glad to share with you: 500 wells across four countries, serving roughly 120,000 people.

## What we accomplished this season

We expect this milestone to change how three regions approach clean water access. Well #500 will mark this chapter of Clearwater's story the way well #1 marked the first one.

The dry season was hard, but our field teams still finished twelve wells in a single month. Experts agree that having clean water within a fifteen-minute walk changes school attendance, and girls in particular. Studies have shown that every dollar spent on water infrastructure returns four dollars in reduced healthcare costs.

Clean water changes a great deal for a village. Every community we've worked with, without exception, has asked us for the same three things: reliability, training, and local ownership.

Whether you're a longtime monthly donor or gave for the first time this year, your gift reached a real family this season.

## Stories from the field

The numbers don't capture what changed in Mbita village this year. This season was hard, too, right up until the rains finally came.

Water used to mean a two-hour walk and missed school days. Now it means a five-minute walk and a full day of class.

The well, finished in March, serves 400 people. Local technicians run the training program that keeps it working, and this year's donors seeded a maintenance fund that covers the next decade.

"This well changed everything for us," one mother told our field team. The team now hears something like that in almost every village we visit. She said her daughters had never missed a full week of school before this year.

Her story leaves us thinking about why a five-minute walk matters so much to her daughters, when the village last went without water, and how many other villages are still waiting for the same thing.

## Looking ahead

Our field coordinator is the link between headquarters and every village crew. The maintenance fund is our insurance against the one thing that could undo a season of work. Next year's plan is our most ambitious yet, and it draws on three years of lessons about what actually keeps a well running.

Next year we'll go from planning to drilling to training within a single dry season, one village at a time. The approach is ambitious but simple, and it's light enough to move quickly while staying durable enough for a decade of hard use.

The plan sets goals for clarity, accountability, and reach in every region we serve.

Some partners have called the approach "unrealistic" for such a short timeline, but three years of our field data says otherwise.

The plan is ready, funded, and moving. We've picked the sites, trained the crews, and ordered the materials, and drilling starts in January.

## Where your support goes

Your gift is split across three areas:

- Drilling: rigs, crews, and the materials that go into the ground
- Training: local technicians who keep every well running for decades
- Monitoring: follow-up visits that catch a failing pump before it fails

This model turns a one-time gift into decades of clean water, and the idea behind it is simple: drill once, train locally, monitor forever. It's lean but complete, so every village gets the support it needs to keep a well running long after we leave.

| Program Area | 2026 Spend | Wells Supported |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| Drilling      | $1.2M      | 500               |
| Training      | $310K      | 500               |

Drilling is our largest line item, and it paid for every well this season. Training costs less, but it kept every well running past year one. Monitoring, the smallest of the three, caught eleven failing pumps before they failed completely.

We started with a single well. We now run a four-country program, and other nonprofits copy our model. We learned three lessons along the way. Drilling without training fails within a year. Monitoring catches problems that training alone misses. And local ownership is the only thing that lasts.
